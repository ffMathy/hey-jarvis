# Sample mode

> Part of [Mobile App](mobile.md). Paths in it are relative to `mobile/` unless they say otherwise.

Before the app is set up there is nothing for the hologram to follow, so the settings screen offers **"No key yet? Try the hologram"**. It opens `sample-screen.tsx`: the same hologram, in a sheet, walking through what Jarvis does. While he is thinking, three pretend agents circle him as satellites, taking turns to call a tool, as a real request's would (`useAgentSatellites(undefined, true)`).

The moods, their order and names, the simulated voice, the mood toast and the frame-rate readout are not this app's: they are `hologram`'s (`sample-mode.ts` and `sample-drive.ts` in the main entry, and `hologram/react/sample`), because the watch's waiting screen is a sample mode too. Only where they sit on the screen is decided here.

**It used to listen to you, and it does not any more.** There was a fourth mood, `microphone`, that opened the phone's microphone and drove the sphere from your own voice — with a recorder of its own on Android (`MicrophoneRecorder`, `AudioRecord` with `VOICE_RECOGNITION`), a browser path through an `AnalyserNode`, a permission prompt, a recording indicator, and an end-to-end emulator check that played a tone in and compared what the app heard against Android's own audio HAL. All of it is gone, along with `sample-voice.ts`, `sample-voice.web.ts`, `UseSampleVoice`, and the emulator harness's `microphone` run.

What it bought was proof that the hologram follows a real voice, which is worth having — but sample mode is a thing to look at before there is an account, and the simulated voices in `hologram/src/simulated-voice.ts` show the same moods from the clock. Every mood is now a tap, and nothing on that screen opens a microphone.

- **A conversation still listens to Jarvis through WebRTC**, which is right: that audio is already inside WebRTC and never touches this path.
- **In a browser it is `getUserMedia` into an `AnalyserNode`** with the ElevenLabs web SDK's settings (`fftSize` 2048, smoothing 0.8), not connected to the speakers.

**All of him is the GPU's.** The aura, the core, the cage, the embers, the fray, the chips and the satellites (`hologram/src/gpu/gpu-picture.ts`) and the body's thousands of fragments and halos (`gpu-body.ts`) are drawn by `hologram/gpu` through expo-gl into one canvas, so a frame costs the UI thread only the calls that issue it, whatever the count. It used to build a Skia picture of everything but the body there, under Hermes, which has no JIT, and the measurements made for that picture were of Skia's path builder, not of a GPU. **Profile the phone before trusting any number here**: none has been read off one.

**The canvas is laid out small and scaled up.** `DRAWN_RESOLUTION` in `hologram/src/react/hologram-view.tsx` lays it out at 0.45 of the screen's resolution and scales it up. It went in when a CPU rasterised the picture — split apart headlessly, painting scaled with area: 384 px cost 49 ms, 269 px 28 ms, 230 px 22 ms — and on the GPU the fragments shaded still scale with it, the body's halos most of all, which is why `SPHERE_FRACTION` counts there too, since a bigger sphere fills more pixels. What a phone's GPU pays for the pixels has not been measured anywhere — SwiftShader emulates one on the CPU, so its GPU timings say nothing about a phone's. **The frame rate is steered, not capped.** `MINIMUM_FRAME_SECONDS` in the view is a safety rail at 1/128 s, not the rate: `hologram/src/density-control.ts` moves the share of the body's particles toward a frame that builds in `BUILD_BUDGET_MS` (8 ms), with `TARGET_FRAMES_PER_SECOND` (40) as a backstop — two windows running below it set a ceiling the count may not climb past. With the body on the GPU a frame builds in well under a millisecond whatever the count, so that backstop is what holds it: the share starts at half (`STARTING_SHARE`) and climbs until the GPU falls behind.

**Sample mode shows the frame rate, how long a picture took to build and the particle count in the corner** (the watch leaves out the build time, for room), counted on the UI thread where the frames happen and read twice a second by a leaf component. It reports what the device achieves, so forty or more means it is holding `TARGET_FRAMES_PER_SECOND` — the least the density loop will settle for — and less is what it managed. The build time is issuing the GPU's frame of all of him, well under a millisecond whatever the count, so it always says there is room and the frame rate is what holds the count — see "His body on the GPU" in [`.claude/rules/hologram-his-body-on-the-gpu-on-a-phone-and-a-watch.md`](hologram-his-body-on-the-gpu-on-a-phone-and-a-watch.md). Use it before and after any change made for speed. **Only sample mode shows it**, on the phone and the watch alike: the conversation screen used to carry a faint copy in a browser, and it was removed at the user's request — a screen that is the assistant has nothing on it but him. The e2e suite checks both halves of that.

**The square is bigger than the screen, and that is nearly free.** What reaches past his limb — the cage, the aura, the embers and the chips he throws on a syllable — is clipped at the edge of the drawing's square, so a square the size of the screen cut it in mid-air. `useWholeScreenHologramSize` makes it half again wider and `SPHERE_FRACTION` is set against that, so the sphere is the same size on screen and the only thing that cuts a chip is the screen itself — where an edge cannot be seen. Measured when Skia drew all but his body, on a CPU rasteriser, that cost 0.6 ms a frame, because it rasterised only what was drawn and everything drawn is sized from the sphere; what the square's area costs a phone's GPU, in his one canvas, has not been measured.

What each layer is, what the voice does to it, and what the footage measured are written at the top of `hologram/src/hologram-drawing.ts`; the look's parts and what the user asked to keep are in [`hologram-what-he-looks-like.md`](hologram-what-he-looks-like.md). These are the things worth knowing before changing it:

- **Speech is activity.** As in the film, what changes while Jarvis talks is behaviour: the specks turn over faster, the hot highlights all but go, the limb frays, more embers drift off, and slabs of light break off the limb on syllable onsets and in the gaps after them. `hologram/src/voice-levels.ts` turns the raw readings into an **agitation** envelope (up over 0.15 s while speech is present, down over 0.15 s, the same for a whisper as for a shout) driving the activity, and into **chip bursts**, which come in flurries of four a quarter-second apart and then rest for 0.6 s. Two departures are the user's: he swells by up to `SWELL_WITH_VOICE`, which is movement rather than light, and he glows by a tenth (`GLOW_WITH_VOICE`).

  **The glow has been asked for in both directions.** `GLOW_WITH_VOICE` brightened the whole drawing with his voice for most of this app's life, because the user could not tell on a phone whether he was talking: it went in at 0.3 and was retuned to 0.8, 2.4, 1.6, 1.1 and 1.35 as each version was looked at on a real screen. On 2026-09-17 the user asked for the brightening gone, since with the sphere most of the screen wide and the sparks drawn out into lines the movement reads on its own, and then for a tenth back. Measured at 0, while Skia drew him, ordinary speech lifted the disc 1–3% and a shout 5–9%, all of it from more lit pixels rather than brighter ones; that has not been measured on the GPU's drawing. The emulator check pins the *holding* — a band, with a floor as well as a ceiling, because a sphere that dimmed while he talked would read as flinching and nothing else would catch it.

  **The fragments now turn over far more slowly than the film's, in both states.** At the film's rate the phone read as a sizzle — the user's word — while Jarvis spoke, "a surface boiling rather than a machine thinking", and what is wanted is his overwhelming calm. The calm pool is about twice as slow as the film's 0.2–0.6 s and the fast pool three to four times slower than it was; measured, that halves the speaking churn and takes a fifth off the idle. It does not take more off the idle however slow the pool gets, because what is left there is the ball *turning*. Do not read the calm as a regression and speed it back up: see `pickFragmentRate`.
- **The body turns like a globe.** The film's interior does not spin, and the drawing once held it pinned for that reason. It turns about the vertical axis once every `BODY_TURN_SECONDS`, the rate the first hologram used, because a ball held still read as inert on a phone; the cage turns with it, and an inner layer of specks counter-turns about the core.
- **The arrival is a vortex, and nothing else.** The film assembles the ball: a point of light, sparks, a band built out of flying pieces, a spoked dial, the fill spreading patch by patch. All of that is gone at the user's request. Instead the sphere is full size from the start and its particles leave the core, nearest first, spiralling out a turn and a half as streaks before settling; the core shows first, and the aura, the cage and the embers are carried out with the shell, over `MATERIALISE_SECONDS` (0.7 s, since it plays on every summoning — it was 1.4 s until the user asked for a shorter arrival). The greeting does not wait for it: "Hello sir" plays the moment he is summoned, during the spin, and the session dials beside it the moment the token is in. It used to be held back until `MATERIALISE_SECONDS` after the summoning, until the user asked for it to play at once. It only moves the particles already being drawn, so the scene's particle count and the density share hold throughout — on the watch as on the phone. A plain fade and growth was tried first and compared side by side; the user chose the vortex.
- **Onsets and gaps need memory, and memory needs `modify`.** `advanceVoiceActivity` writes into a state object that lives from frame to frame, and it is fed the *raw* reading rather than the eased one, because easing is exactly what smears a syllable's start into a slope. The view advances it inside `activity.modify(...)`, the way the bands are eased: a shared value assigned from the JS runtime gets a warning-only setter in the UI runtime, so writing to it directly would drop every update in silence in a development build and work in release. `hologram/src/voice-levels.ts`'s header says so at length.
- **It spirals out of its core.** For the first `MATERIALISE_SECONDS` after the canvas mounts, `appearance = min(1, time / MATERIALISE_SECONDS)`, which the view computes from its own clock, runs the vortex. Nothing is drawn over it while it does.
- **It is all worklets.** `analyseFrame`, every per-fragment helper and the GPU's draw functions start with `'worklet'` and use only their arguments and module-level number constants — no mutable module state, no closures, no `Math.random` while drawing; randomness comes from the seeded scene. The scene (flat number arrays, built once from a fixed seed) and what the GPU is given (programs, buffers and textures, made once) belong to one mounted canvas and are never shared between two.
- **Loudness is eased on the UI thread, not the JS thread.** The SDK refreshes about 25 times a second; easing toward each reading every frame is what keeps the bands smooth, and `easeLevel` is exponential so the result is the same on a 60 Hz and a 120 Hz screen. The tracker is frame-rate independent for the same reason, and tested at 30, 60, 90 and 144 Hz.
- **It was designed by looking, and is tested by looking.** What he looks like drawn — that he moves when silent, that speech shows as chips beyond the limb and more change than when calm, that his core ring rolls and his cage turns with his body, that he arrives as a vortex out of his core and nothing else, and that he stays inside his square — needs a GPU, so it is the headset's browser tests' to say, on the very picture the apps draw (`vr/tests/e2e/`). `hologram/src/hologram-drawing.spec.ts` covers the scene and what a frame of him is, and this app's `tests/e2e/hologram-canvas.spec.ts` reads his canvas back in Chromium. The device check is `.scripts/verify-hologram-on-emulator.sh`, below.
