# The conversation

> Part of [Wear OS App](watch.md). Paths in it are relative to `watch/` unless they say otherwise.

The same ElevenLabs agent the phone talks to, over WebRTC, opened the moment the screen is. There is no button, no status line and no title — the argument is the phone conversation screen's, only more so, since a round 45 mm display has nowhere to put any of it. What tells you which of you is talking is what the sphere is doing.

**He answers in the phone's earbuds when the phone has some.** A watch can't play into a headset connected to its phone, because Android has no such route. So before anything is played or recorded, the watch sends the phone a Data Layer *request* on `/jarvis/answer-on-the-phone` and waits up to three seconds (`ASK_THE_PHONE_MS`). The phone (`JarvisWatchSummonService` in `mobile/modules/jarvis-assistant`) says yes only when all of these hold: a Bluetooth headset is connected, Jarvis is its assistant (locked or not — his window stays up over the lock screen), and its assistant window has actually come up. That window is exactly what the phone's own gesture opens, so the conversation goes to the AirPods the way it always does there. The watch then holds no conversation and shows no line about it: he stays on the screen and moves as the phone's sphere does. For as long as the phone's window is up it sends the watch a frame of what its sphere is handed every 80 ms on `/jarvis/mirror-the-phone` — whether he is talking and how loud, his spectrum folded to the drawing's 24 bands, the listener's presence and level, and whether he is thinking (`useWatchMirror` in `mobile/src/use-watch-mirror.ts`, `WatchMirror.kt` in `mobile/modules/jarvis-assistant`) — and the watch draws from those (`phone-mirror.ts`). The frame's layout is `mirrored-sphere.ts` in `hologram`, one file for both ends. The window being put away sends an empty frame, and a watch that hears nothing for 1.5 s brings him to rest anyway. Any other answer, or none, and the watch talks for itself. The phone gives its window two seconds, less than the watch waits, so that the watch doesn't give up on a phone that is about to say yes. `watch-link.contract.spec.ts` checks the two timeouts against each other. A phone window that opens after its own two seconds can't be taken back, and then both of them talk. None of this has run on hardware. The open questions are whether `showSession` from a background request opens the window with the phone's screen off, and whether the phone's microphone is allowed in that state.

**He hangs up when it goes quiet.** Once a request is finished and nobody answers, the agent ends the
call itself, with its `turnTimeout` of three seconds and its `end_call` tool; the watch registers no
client tool for it. To the watch it is the agent hanging up: the network is let go and he stays on
the screen until the wrist drops.

**On the watch's own speaker he is at 90% of the call volume.** Everything he says there is call audio, and full call volume from a wrist carries across a room. `modules/jarvis-volume` lowers `STREAM_VOICE_CALL` to 90% of its maximum before the greeting, in the watch's own volume steps, rounded down so a five-step scale really comes down one step. It only ever lowers the volume: a wearer who set it lower keeps their setting.

Two things differ from the phone, both deliberate:

- **His voice comes from the SDK's analysers**, not from a tap on the WebRTC track: the watch hands the session no room check and no way to follow his track, so it reads the SDK's readings, and the note on the conversation screen says why: tapping the track means a native module, a peer-connection id and a ring buffer, and on a sphere this size the SDK's readings are good enough. The phone keeps its tap.
- **A refused microphone is the end of it.** The phone falls back to a text field in a browser, where there is a keyboard in front of you. Here the assistant gesture *is* the request to be talked to.

**The conversation is the phone's and the headset's session** (`useJarvisSession` in `hologram/conversation` over `createJarvisSession` in `hologram`; see "One conversation, three devices" in [`.claude/rules/hologram-one-conversation-three-devices.md`](hologram-one-conversation-three-devices.md)). What the watch hands it is only its own: its name in the history (`jarvis-wear`), getting off the phone's Bluetooth before the token (`holdFastNetwork` as `untilOnline`, `releaseFastNetwork` as `leaveNetwork`), and what the deadline says. Asking the phone, the microphone and the call volume happen on the screen before he is summoned.

**He greets you before he is connected**, exactly as the phone does: "Hello sir, how can I help?" plays from the firmware's recording as soon as the microphone is granted, inside the call's audio, and everything else — getting off the Bluetooth proxy, the token — happens while he says it, with the agent's first message switched off; the session is dialled behind him the moment the token is in, muted until he has finished, so the agent never hears the greeting through a speaker centimetres from the microphone. Dropping the wrist mid-greeting stops it. The listening lattice comes from the session's `user`, as on the phone. An error once the conversation is open no longer stops the sphere following him, or lets the network go under a call still running; the twenty-second deadline, counted from the moment the network is up, ends a conversation that connects after it. None of this has been heard on a watch yet: whether the greeting survives LiveKit switching the audio mode for the call mid-sentence is the first thing a real one should settle.

**A conversation is held over Wi-Fi or cellular, never the phone's Bluetooth.** Near its phone a watch is online through a Bluetooth proxy that Wear OS prefers because it is cheap to keep up, and it carries TCP only. WebRTC's audio is UDP, so on that network the token request works and the session can even say it is connected, while no audio gets through either way — which is how the watch first behaved: Jarvis turned and said nothing. Before each conversation `modules/jarvis-network` asks for Wi-Fi or cellular with `requestNetwork` and binds the process to it, which is Google's own advice for anything that streams on a watch, and lets it go when the conversation ends or the wrist drops. If neither comes up within a few seconds the conversation is tried anyway, and when it then fails to open, the line over him says to put the watch on Wi-Fi.

`@livekit/react-native-expo-plugin` is configured with `audioType: 'communication'`, which is not a nicety: without it the native audio session is set up for media playback, the microphone routes to the speaker, and Jarvis hears himself back. On a watch the speaker and the microphone are centimetres apart, so that is not a subtle failure.
